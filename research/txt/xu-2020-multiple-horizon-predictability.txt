    Research in finance and macroeconomics has routinely employed multiple horizons to test
    asset return predictability. In a simple predictive regression model, we find the popular
    scaled test can have zero power when the predictor is not sufficiently persistent. A new test
    based on implication of the short-run model is suggested and is shown to be uniformly more
    powerful than the scaled test. The new test can accommodate multiple predictors. Compared
    with various other widely used tests, simulation experiments demonstrate remarkable finite-
    sample performance. We reexamine the predictive ability of various popular predictors for
    aggregate equity premium. (JEL C32, C53, C58, G12, G14, G17)

The predictability in asset returns is often examined beyond one forecast
horizon. Fama and French (1988) and Campbell and Shiller (1988) found
that accumulated short-run predictability can be the dominant influence on
longer-run movements in asset prices. Ang and Bekaert (2007), Cochrane
(2008), Lettau and van Nieuwerburgh (2008), Bansal and Shaliastovich (2013),
and Rapach, Ringgenberg, and Zhou (2016) are recent studies of return
predictability in which analyses over multiple horizons (or even at an infinite
horizon) play a prominent role. Long horizon predictability tests are also useful
in macroeconomics, for example, to detect predictable components in interest
rates, inflation, and exchange rates.1

predictability literature. Unless the predictor is strictly exogenous, strong

  formed under the assumption of weak dependence. This issue has motivated
  several recent influential studies in the setting of the standard (or short-run,
  single-horizon) predictive regression.2 In the multiple-horizon regression, our
  primary focus in this article, the outcome variable (e.g., multiple-period returns)
  is the aggregation of the single-horizon outcome. The potential effects of
  the predictor's persistence on inference are even further amplified because
  the outcome is overlapping, and thus also dependent. A large amount of
  empirical results favoring predictability over longer horizons are based on
  the test using the Newey-West (1987) (or Hansen-Hodrick (1980)) standard
  error. The Newey-West test can seriously overreject in finite samples due to
  the interaction between the persistent regressor and serially correlated errors.
  The standard error proposed by Hodrick (1992), section 1), which uses the
  moving-average structure of the aggregated error under the null hypothesis (of
  nonpredictability), performs better in finite samples (Ang and Bekaert 2007).
  However, the construction relies on weak dependence. Wei and Wright (2013)
  derive the asymptotic distribution of Hodrick's test (which is a nonstandard
  distribution) with strongly dependent regressors.

     One approach that accommodates strongly persistent predictors is the scaled
  test, proposed by Valkanov (2003) and Hjalmarsson (2011). They use the usual
  t-statistic divided by the square root of the horizon (referred to as the scaled
  t-statistic) to reflect inflation of the standard error as the horizon increases,
  and the procedure has gained popularity among practitioners. However, the
  scale is motivated by strong persistence, and as we demonstrate, it tends to
  overestimate the standard error if the predictor is just weakly dependent. Using
  the asymptotics that allow for long horizons and various degrees of persistence,
  we show that under the null of nonpredictability, the scaled t-statistic of
  Hjalmarsson (2011) converges to zero if h(1-)  , where h is the forecast
  horizon and  is the autoregressive slope of the predictor. Consequently, under
  the alternative, the scaled t-statistic converges to zero if n-1/2h(1-)1/2  ,
  where n is the sample size. This means that with a weakly dependent predictor,
  the test has zero power if the horizon increases faster than n1/2.

     The practical implication is that regardless of whether predictability is
  detected in the first few horizons, the significance (if any) of the scaled test
  will completely disappear as the horizon increases, if the predictor is not very
  strongly persistent. This is also the pattern we found in our empirical analysis.

     In this paper, we pursue a different approach to infer about the multiple-
  horizon predictability slope through the implication of the short-run model.
  This method does not estimate the multiple-horizon regression directly, and

   In the simulation experiments with empirically relevant designs, we confirm
that the substantial power improvement of the implied test carries over to finite
samples, and we provide evidence for its appealing performance over various
other popular tests.

   Recent work by Phillips and Lee (2013) and Kostakis et al. (2015, section
5) also propose robust tests, both based on the idea of constructing mildly
persistent instrumental variables. Importantly, both studies take an indirect
approach. Phillips and Lee (2013) estimate the backward multiple-horizon
regression, in which the parameter of interest is different from ours and
coincides only under nonpredictability. The test of Kostakis, Magdalinos, and
Stamatogiannis (2015) uses long-horizon returns as the dependent variable but
the construction is built on a different estimand from ours.

   Our approach thus complements theirs. An important benefit is that we
focus directly on the multiple-horizon slope (as defined by h in Equation
(4)) of the predictive regression in which only the outcome is aggregated and
the predictor remains the same with the usual short-run predictive regression.
Thus, our approach is able to offer a confidence interval for the parameter
of dramatic economic meaning, the multiple-horizon slope and, thereby, the
multiple-horizon expected returns. For example, Rapach, Ringgenberg, and
Zhou (2016) consider a mean-variance investor who rebalances his/her portfolio
at the frequency lower than the data frequency (e.g., managing asset allocation
quarterly (h = 3) or annually (h = 12), given monthly observations). Thus, the
investor's asset allocation strategy is based on multiple-horizon expected
returns and variances. Moreover, our test requires no tuning parameter. By
contrast, as Phillips (2014) notes, the instrumental-variable-based approach
requires the user's input of a key tuning parameter (which determines how mild
the persistence of the instrumental variable is), but a satisfactory data-dependent
selection rule is currently lacking.

multiple-horizon slope. They propose running the reverse-regression and then

5.2 The tests
For the univariate predictive regression model, we consider five tests in the
experiments. All tests are one sided. The first two are long-horizon-regression-
based t-tests using the Newey-West standard error (with h-1 being the
truncation point) and the standard error proposed by Hodrick (1992, section 1),
both of which are popular in empirical studies. Both tests use standard normal
critical values.

   The next two are the implied t-test TIM(0) and the long-horizon-regression-
based scaled test, which are the focus of our asymptotic analysis. For the
scaled test, we implement the test proposed by Hjalmarsson (2011), who uses a
endogeneity correction and Bonferroni critical values, and we thus refer to it as
Hjalmarsson's test. Hjalmarsson's test statistic is asymptotically normal, and
thus should be compared with the one-sided N (0,1) critical value (e.g., 1.645,
if the test is right-tailed). We also implemented the test based on the t-statistic
TScaled(0) without the endogeneity correction, using the robust confidence
interval for c (Remark 4). We find that although it has better size control (in
particular, under DGP D), but it is overall less powerful than Hjalmarsson's test.
Nevertheless, the difference is rather small and importantly, most properties of
the scaled test, which we will discuss below, remain unchanged (Xu 2018).

   For the proposed implied test, we use the bias-corrected bootstrap critical
values, following Algorithm 1 and Remark A1 in Appendix A. In a working
paper version (Xu 2018), we also implement the implied test with two other
methods of obtaining critical values, one based on the Bonferroni procedure,

5.3.1 Newey-West and Hodrick tests. The Newey-West test works poorly
under DGP A, which mimics the dynamics of returns and dividend-to-price
ratios (Figure 2a). The empirical size (null rejection rate) is approximately
16%-30% for a test with nominal size 5% for horizons one to five, and the
size distortion grows with the horizon. When h = 1, the Newey-West t-statistic
reduces to the White t-statistic, and the overrejection is well understood from its
non-Gaussian limit distribution (with some extension of the results in Campbell
and Yogo (2006)), because of strong persistence of the predictor interacting
with strong negative endogeneity. Figure 2a shows that the size distortion even
worsens as the horizon increases.

   For DGPs B and C, the Newey-West test has a size close to 5% when h = 1,
since minor endogeneity almost makes the test asymptotically normal, and thus
approximately valid even with strong persistence. The overrejection starts to
pick up when the horizon increases, although it is much milder than that under
DGP A, with the largest size about 15%. For DGP D, the Newey-West test has
the size close to 5% for all horizons considered since the predictor has low
persistence.

   Why can the Newey-West test perform poorly over horizons? It is well
known that the test overrejects seriously when serial correlation in xt-1u0t (h)
(estimated by xt-1u0t (h)) is strong (Stock and Watson 2011). This exactly
happens quickly when the horizon increases, as long as the predictor is not
very weakly dependent (like DGP D). This happens even when xt-1u0t (h)
is almost serially uncorrelated for h = 1, by construction. Figure 17 in the next
section plots first-order autocorrelations of xt-1u0t (h) against h for the samples
in the empirical analysis, which showcases this observation.

   The Hodrick test, a favorite among empiricists (Hodrick 1992; Ang and
Bekaert 2007; Bakshi, Panayotov, and Skoulakis 2011), coincides with the
Newey-West test when h = 1. Under DGP A, it has obvious size distortion
when h = 1, as the Newey-West test, but the size distortion does not increase

                                                                                                                                                                                       4421
The Review of Financial Studies / v 33 n 9 2020                                       Downloaded from academic.oup.com/rfs/article/33/9/4403/5620729 by Lancaster University user on 12 September 2026

much over horizons. The empirical size is approximately between 15% and 19%
over horizons, which still causes concerns when interpreting empirical results
on the basis of this test. We also find that under this DGP, the distribution of the
Hodrick test statistic is skewed to the right, so a two-sided test tends to obscure
the difference and have less size distortion. The Hodrick test works well under
DGPs B and C (low endogeneity) over horizons, with minor size distortion,
and works almost perfectly under DGP D (low persistence).

   As a result of lack of size control under the null, the Newey-West test also
has higher rejection rates under the alternative hypothesis, than other tests
considered. We include its power curve for completeness.

5.3.2 Hjalmarsson's scaled test. We now discuss three more recent tests.
Hjalmarsson's scaled test maintains the size control below 5% for all
horizons under DGPs A-C, but it's obviously conservative, in particular when
endogeneity is strongly negative (Figure 2a, DGP A) or the horizon is long
(Figure 6a, DGP C). This is consistent with the findings of the simulation study
in Hjalmarsson (2011), table 2, p. 828), which mostly focuses on persistent
predictors.

   Under DGP D, where the persistence of the predictor is low, Hjalmarsson's
test overrejects with a finite-sample size 8.8% when h = 1. This is probably due
to the incorrect coverage of the confidence interval for the AR slope based on
inverting a unit root test, if the underlying process is not persistent, as pointed
out by Phillips (2014). Strikingly, under this DGP D, the size falls to almost
zero for any multiple horizon h > 1 (Figure 8a).

   The undersized behavior of the scaled test we observe in most cases causes
concerns for potential low power, which is confirmed in our analysis. This
behavior originates from two sources. First, the Bonferroni procedure is
conservative by nature. Second, more importantly, the scaling factor is too
large when the predictor is not very persistent, which is a new finding in this
paper. The effect is most obvious at a long horizon.

   As a result, the scaled test has the lowest power among all tests considered in
almost all cases (except for DGP D at h = 1, where the test has incorrect size), in
many cases by a quite large margin. For example, under DGP A, when  = 0.08,
the power of the scaled test is between 22.2% and 27.8% over all horizons, while
the implied test proposed in this paper has power between 51.3% and 57.3%
over horizons (Figure 2c). Under DGPs B and C, while the scaled test has
similar or only slightly lower power compared with other tests at the first few
horizons, there is obviously decreasing trend of the power when the horizon
increases, and the power disadvantage is much enlarged at longer horizons. For
DGP D, under which the scaled test has the most dismaying performance, the
power quickly drops to almost zero when the horizon is beyond the first few
(Figures 8b and 8c). This confirms our prediction in the asymptotic analysis
(Theorem 2); the test statistic itself converges to zero (thereby passing the

   We also consider the scaled test with the robust confidence interval for the
AR slope, suggested by Mikusheva (2007) and Phillips (2014). The modified
scaled test has a better size control regardless of the persistence level of the
predictor, but it remains to have low power as Hjalmarsson's test (Xu 2018).

5.3.3 Implied test and KMS test. The KMS test and the implied test perform
well overall. Under DGP A, both tests have some size distortion (between 8.3%
and 9% for KMS test, and between 7.2% and 8.5% for the implied test) over
horizons, probably because of the relatively small sample size. Under other
DGPs, the two tests have the size close to the nominal level 5% over all horizons
considered.

   Both KMS and implied tests have substantially higher power than the scaled
test for all DGPs considered, across almost all multiple horizons. Furthermore,
the implied test has certain degree of power advantage over the KMS test.
Under DGP A, the KMS and implied tests have very close power. The power
gain of the implied test over the KMS test exists noticeably for DGPs B, C,
and D, in particular, at multiple horizons. For example, under DGP C (Figure
6), when  = -0.002, the power of the implied test increases from 23.4% to
27.1% from h = 1 to h = 48 (monthly observations), while the power of the KMS
test decreases from 22.7% to 10.9%. For a farther alternative  = -0.005, the
power of the implied test increases from 74.9% to 78.1% over horizons, while
the power of the KMS test decreases from 72.4% to 32.9%. Under DGP D, the
power of the KMS is very low and shrinks almost to the empirical size as the
horizon increases, while the implied test maintains the power around 26% for
 = 0.2 (Figure 8b) and around 80% for  = 0.5 (Figure 8c).

   We notice that while the power curve against h has a monotonically
decreasing pattern for the KMS test under all DGPs considered, the power curve
is relatively stable for the implied test across horizons. Kostakis, Magdalinos,
and Stamatogiannis (2015, section 5) obtain similar results in their simulations
regarding the KMS test.

5.3.4 Robustness analysis. We now evaluate the performance of various tests
if the DGP allows some serial correlation in the error of predictor dynamics
uxt . We consider four additional DGPs (denoted as DGPs A.ROB-D.ROB),
similar to DGPs A-D, respectively, except that uxt is allowed to be serially
correlated, following an AR(1) model. The AR(1) slope of uxt is empirically
determined, using the data set underlying each of DGPs A-D. These slopes are
small, taking values -0.056, -0.102, -0.157, and 0.002 for DGPs A.ROB-
D.ROB, respectively. Other parameters are also empirically determined, and
they are slightly different from those for DGPs A-D. The Online Appendix
gives details.

6.2.2 Data set II. Now we turn to Data set II with quarterly observations.
For 4 persistent predictors (CAY, DP, DY, and TBL), the pattern of slopes of
LH regressions generally follows that of slopes estimated through implication
(Figures 18 and 20), though two estimators tend to deviate from each other for
longer horizons (in particular, for DY and TBL). For predictors with nearly no
serial correlation (DFR and LTR), two estimators perform very differently; the
implied estimators stabilize quickly as the horizon increases, and the slopes
stay rather flat over the whole range of horizons. This is expected, as the added
amount of predictability is very small over multiple horizons if the predictor
itself is serially almost uncorrelated. In these cases (DFR and LTR), however,
the LH regression slopes behave wildly across horizons (Figures 20b and 20c),
probably because of the large estimation uncertainty induced by the large
variance accumulated in the regression error u0t (h) across horizons (which does
not occur for the implied estimator). Figures 22b and 22c depict the slopes of
these two predictors using Data set III, and the similar observation follows.

   The R2s of LH regressions (Figures 18d and 20d) increase with horizon for
persistent predictors, but they largely decrease with horizon for predictors with
almost no serial correlation. This corroborates our asymptotic analysis (Remark
1).

strong predictor in this data set. The only evidence for predictability at the 5%

    6.2.6 Summary. Our results show that even with more powerful tests (against
    one-sided or two-sided alternatives), the widely used predictor the dividend-
    to-price ratio presents quite weak predictive ability for market returns when
    the persistence is properly accommodated, even at long horizons for which the
    strongest evidence typically occurs. Other predictors such as the consumption-
    wealth ratio, the short interest and the corporate-government bond returns
    spread provide stronger evidence than the dividend-to-price ratio, and the
    evidence is fairly coherent over various horizons.

7. Conclusion

    Foreseeability of the price of stocks and bonds over longer (instead of shorter)
    periods was one of the key findings of the Laureates of the 2013 Nobel prize in
    economic sciences (Pressmeddelande 2013). Recent research on appropriate
    statistical inference procedures for long-horizon predictability has focused
    on accommodation of strong persistence in predictors. The current paper
    demonstrates a potential pitfall of the popular test based on the scaled t-statistic,
    which uses a too large standard error when the predictor is not persistent. The
    test based on the implication of the short-run model circumvents the necessity
    of dealing with the aggregated error, which is strongly dependent as the horizon
    increases. Unlike the scaled test, the implication-based test uses the persistence-
    adaptive standard error. Coupled with the bias-corrected bootstrap procedure
    we suggest, the implication-based test has attractive properties, as we elaborate
    in the paper, and is thus recommended.

       The validity of the test hinges on specification of the autocorrelation structure
    of the predictor. Such specification has been proved fruitful in the literature
    on short-run predictability to design tests against unknown persistence of
    the predictor, and it underlies several popular robust procedures, prominently
    those of Campbell and Yogo (2006) and Phillips (2014). The alternative
    bias-correction approach (to short-run predictability), proposed by Stambaugh
    (1999), Amihud and Hurvich (2004), and Amihud et al. (2009), also relies on
    the correct specification of predictors' autocorrelation structure. Although we
    demonstrate that the level of mild misspecification in the empirical analysis is
    mostly harmless, an important direction for future work is to allow for flexible
    specification of the predictor dynamics.
