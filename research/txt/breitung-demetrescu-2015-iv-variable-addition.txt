Instrumental variable and variable addition based inference in
predictive regressions

Jörg Breitung a, Matei Demetrescu b,

a University of Cologne, Institute of Econometrics and Statistics, Albertus-Magnus-Platz D-50923 Köln, Germany

article info                    abstract

Article history:                Valid inference in predictive regressions depends in a crucial manner on the degree of persistence of the
Received 22 February 2012       predictor variables. The paper studies test procedures that are robust in the sense that their asymptotic
Received in revised form        null distributions are invariant to the persistence of the predictor, that is, the limiting distribution is the
28 October 2013                 same irrespective of whether the regressors are stationary or (nearly) integrated. Existing procedures are
Accepted 29 October 2013        often conservative (e.g. tests based on Bonferroni bounds), are based on highly restrictive assumptions
Available online 14 March 2015  (such as homoskedasticity or assuming an AR(1) process for the regressor) or fail to have power against

JEL classification:             alternatives in a 1/T neighborhood of the null hypothesis. We first propose a refinement of the variable
C12
C32                             addition method with improved asymptotic power approaching the optimal rate. Second, inference based
                                on instrumental variables may further improve the (local) power of the test and even achieve local power
Keywords:
Causality test                  under the optimal 1/T rate. We give high-level conditions under which the suggested variable addition and
Persistence
Integration                     instrumental variable procedures are valid no matter whether the predictor is stationary, near-integrated
Long memory                     or integrated, or exhibits time-varying volatility. All test statistics possess a standard limiting distribution.
IV estimation                   Monte Carlo experiments suggest that tests based on simple combinations of instruments perform most
                                promising relative to existing tests. An application to quarterly US stock returns illustrates the need for
                                robust inference.
